\title{Elastic response of aluminium to magnetostriction of an Al$_{13}$Fe$_4$
inclusion and the extent of possible plasticity}
\author{I.~Mikhailovskiy\thanks{Corresponding author.
\texttt{ilyamihailovsy@gmail.com}} \and D.~Pshonkin}
\date{Moscow Polytechnic University, Moscow, Russia}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Molecular dynamics at 0~K gives stresses around a spherical
Al$_{13}$Fe$_4$ inclusion of radius 2.5~nm in aluminium, lengthened by
magnetostriction along the magnetic field and shortened across it. The
matrix is compressed by 126~MPa at the ends of the inclusion and
stretched by 75~MPa at its sides; shear stresses reach
28~MPa. The response is elastic: tenfold strain forms no
dislocations, and a dislocation above the inclusion shifts
0.78~\AA{} and returns on unloading. A compliant interface layer
makes the atomistic stresses about 80\% of the elastic solution.
Around a micron-size particle the elastic stress exceeds a yield stress of
40--60~MPa within about one radius, and one of 120--151~MPa only in a thin
surface layer. With the activation volume
measured for Al--Mg--Si, the observed creep increase would need 6--7 times
the measured Fe--Al magnetostriction. Why the effect outlasts the field
remains unexplained.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords}\quad molecular dynamics; magnetostriction;
inclusions; internal stress; aluminium alloys

\section{Introduction}

Al--Mg--Si alloys containing iron-rich inclusions creep faster after they
have been held in a static magnetic field (creep is slow deformation under a
constant load), and the effect persists after the field is removed
\cite{Skvortsov2019JAP,Skvortsov2019PSS,Friha2024JMMM,Nikolaev2025SciRep}.
After holding in a 0.7~T field the creep rate rises by up to 25\% and the
microhardness falls by up to 30\% \cite{Skvortsov2019JAP}. The extra strain
of the samples grows linearly with the induction between 0.2 and 0.7~T
\cite{Friha2024JMMM}, and the heat released in the subsequent deformation
grows fivefold \cite{Pshonkin2025ZL}. The inclusions are 1--5~$\mu$m in size
\cite{Skvortsov2019JAP,Pshonkin2025ZL}.

One proposed explanation is magnetostriction of the inclusions, the change of
their shape in the field. The matrix resists the change, and stresses appear
around the inclusion that can move dislocations, the line defects whose
motion carries plastic deformation. The torque with which the field turns a
magnetised particle gives only about 0.1~MPa at the interface
\cite{Skvortsov2019JAP}. The same papers estimate the magnetostrictive stress
as 147~MPa \cite{Friha2024JMMM}, 560~MPa \cite{Skvortsov2019JAP} and
650~MPa \cite{Friha2023PSS}. Each estimate is a single number: it does not
say how the stress is distributed around the particle, how fast it falls
with distance, or how much of it acts on dislocations.

Here the stress field around one Al$_{13}$Fe$_4$ inclusion in aluminium is
calculated by molecular dynamics, which follows the atoms under the forces
between them. The forces come from an interatomic potential (a formula for
the energy of interacting atoms). The potential does not describe magnetism,
so the strain of the inclusion is prescribed; its magnitude corresponds to
the 147~MPa estimate. The atomistic result is compared with the elastic
solution, and the field is then used to estimate the force on dislocations
and the increase in creep rate. The field of the thermoelastic stresses that
an electric current produces around such inclusions was computed earlier by
the finite-element method \cite{Friha2023PSS}.

The question addressed here is whether the magnetostriction of an
inclusion can by itself deform aluminium plastically, that is, create
dislocations or move them irreversibly, and if so, in what zone around the
particle. The calculation shows that at the given strain the response of
aluminium is elastic, and plastic flow is possible only in a layer next to
the particle whose thickness depends on the yield stress of the alloy.

\section{Model and method}

\subsection{The cell}

The cell is a periodic box of aluminium, $12.6\times12.4\times12.6$~nm.
A spherical Al$_{13}$Fe$_4$ inclusion of radius $R=2.5$~nm sits at its centre
(Fig.~\ref{fig:cell}a). The inclusion is cut from an Al$_{13}$Fe$_4$ crystal
with the lattice of the crystal-structure data \cite{Liu2024CIF}; aluminium
atoms closer than 2.2~\AA{} to an inclusion atom are removed. The cell holds
118\,861 atoms, 114\,374 in the matrix and 4\,487 in the inclusion. Aluminium surrounds the inclusion on all sides. The boundary
conditions are periodic along all three axes, so the cell represents a
regular array of inclusions 12.6~nm apart along $x$ and $z$ and 12.4~nm along $y$; their volume fraction is 3.3\%.

A sphere was chosen as the simplest compact shape; the actual shape of the
inclusions is not reproduced. The stress
around a sphere falls with the distance $r$ from its centre as
$r^{-3}$ and is small two or three radii away, so a cell of $10^5$ atoms is
large enough. A sphere also has an exact elastic solution
\cite{Eshelby1957,Mura1987} against which the atomistic result can be checked.

The cell axes are $x\parallel[1\bar10]$, $y\parallel[11\bar2]$ and
$z\parallel[111]$. The $(111)$ planes on which dislocations glide in
aluminium are then horizontal, and the slip direction $[1\bar10]$ lies along
$x$. The interatomic potential for Al--Si--Mg--Cu--Fe is taken from
Ref.~\cite{Jelinek2012} (modified embedded-atom method); the calculations use
LAMMPS \cite{Thompson2022LAMMPS}. The elastic constants of aluminium with
this potential, $C_{11}=110.5$, $C_{12}=60.9$ and $C_{44}=28.4$~GPa, are close
to the measured ones.

\subsection{The given magnetostrictive strain}
\label{sec:strain}

The magnetic field points along $x$. Magnetostriction lengthens the inclusion
along the field and shortens it across, without changing its volume
(Fig.~\ref{fig:cell}b):
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon_{xx}=\varepsilon,\qquad
\varepsilon_{yy}=\varepsilon_{zz}=-\varepsilon/2,\qquad
\varepsilon=1.94\times10^{-3}.
\label{eq:strain}
\end{equation}
This $\varepsilon$ comes from the 147~MPa estimate of Ref.~\cite{Friha2024JMMM}.
That estimate is the Young's modulus of aluminium, 70~GPa, times the extra
strain that the samples accumulate in creep after holding in a 0.7~T field
(about 0.21\%); the Young's modulus is the stress needed per unit strain.
Here 147~MPa is divided by the Young's modulus measured on the alloy
samples, 75.7~GPa \cite{Pshonkin2025ZL}. The magnetostriction of the
Al$_{13}$Fe$_4$ inclusions themselves has not been measured, so
$\varepsilon$ is an input of the calculation. Every stress below is
proportional to $\varepsilon$; for another strain $\varepsilon'$ it is
multiplied by $\varepsilon'/0.194\%$. With the same modulus, the estimates
of 560 and 650~MPa \cite{Skvortsov2019JAP,Friha2023PSS} correspond to
strains of 0.74--0.86\%, 3.8 and 4.4 times the given one; the calculations at
$4\varepsilon$ and $10\varepsilon$ (Supplementary S2) cover this range.

Each inclusion atom is moved by
$\mathbf u=\boldsymbol\varepsilon(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_c)$, where $\mathbf r$ is
the position of the atom before the move and $\mathbf r_c$ the inclusion
centre, and is then held fixed. The aluminium atoms move until the forces on
them vanish (energy minimisation). This gives the inclusion the shape it is
assumed to take under the field. The arrows in Fig.~\ref{fig:cell}b show the
forces with which the inclusion acts on the aluminium (single-sphere elastic
solution): outward at the ends along the field and inward on top, at the
bottom and at the sides. In a real inclusion magnetic forces would maintain
this shape, if a magnetostriction of this size exists.

The strain (\ref{eq:strain}) is the strain of the inclusion inside the matrix.
A free particle would lengthen more in the same field, because no matrix
would hold it back. If 0.194\% were the free magnetostriction of a particle
two to two and a half times stiffer than aluminium, every stress would be
about one third smaller than computed (Supplementary S3).

The reference cell, without strain, is prepared once. The inclusion atoms are
fixed from the start: with this potential the equilibrium lattice of
Al$_{13}$Fe$_4$ is 8\% larger in volume than the measured one, and a free
inclusion would swell. The matrix is held at 300~K for 10~ps, cooled to
0.5~K and relaxed at 0~K; the cell size is then adjusted so that the mean
stress in the aluminium far from the inclusion vanishes. The cells with
strain $+\varepsilon$ and $-\varepsilon$ are built from the reference cell
by moving the inclusion atoms, at fixed cell size. Comparing the responses
to $+\varepsilon$ and $-\varepsilon$ shows how far the response departs from
linear (Supplementary S2).

\subsection{How the stresses are obtained}

The stress at each matrix atom is computed from the forces of its
neighbours (the virial of the atom) and divided by the atomic volume of
aluminium, 16.61~\AA$^3$; in a uniform crystal the mean of these atomic
stresses is the ordinary stress. The quantity used is the difference
between the strained cell and the reference cell, that is, the stress added
by the magnetostriction. Tension is positive and compression negative.
Three quantities are used. The normal stress along the radius,
$\sigma_{rr}$, shows how hard the matrix pushes on the inclusion or pulls
it; the estimates of 147--650~MPa are of this kind. The equivalent (von
Mises) stress, $\sigma_{\text{eq}}$, combines all components except the
hydrostatic pressure into one number that is compared with the yield stress,
the stress at which the alloy starts to deform plastically. The shear stress
$\tau=\sigma_{xz}$ shears the crystal along the horizontal slip plane in the
$x$ direction and so pushes an edge dislocation with Burgers vector along
$x$. The Burgers vector is the shift of the crystal left behind when the
dislocation passes; for an edge dislocation it is perpendicular to the line.

Values are averaged over atoms in spherical shells 2~\AA{} thick inside
narrow cones about chosen directions: along the field, across it, and at
25$^\circ$ and 67$^\circ$ to the field, where the shear stress is largest
(within 10$^\circ$ of the direction, 7.5$^\circ$ for the inclined ones). The
distance $d=r-R$ is measured from the inclusion surface. For the maps in
Fig.~\ref{fig:map} the stresses are averaged over rotations about the field
axis: the field is symmetric about it apart from the weak dependence of the
elasticity of aluminium on direction.

\subsection{The elastic solution}

Elasticity theory treats the metal as a continuous elastic medium without
atoms. The atomistic result is compared with two of its solutions. The first
is exact: a single sphere with the same strain in an infinite isotropic
matrix \cite{Eshelby1957,Mura1987,JuSun1999}. The second is computed
numerically for the same periodic cell \cite{MoulinecSuquet1998} with the
cubic elastic constants of aluminium from the potential. It includes the
neighbouring inclusions and the fixed cell size, so it can be compared with
the atomistic result directly. The first shows the field of a single
inclusion in a large grain and carries over to particles of real size.

\section{Results}

\subsection{How the stress is distributed}

Fig.~\ref{fig:map} shows how the added stress is distributed around the
inclusion. Along the field, at the ends of the inclusion, the matrix is
compressed: the inclusion pushes it apart. Across the field the matrix is in
tension: the inclusion draws back from it. The shear stress on the slip planes
vanishes on the $x$ and $z$ axes and is largest in four inclined lobes, at
about 25$^\circ$ and 67$^\circ$ to the field, changing sign from lobe to lobe.
The atomistic map and the elastic map for the same cell agree in shape, but
the atomistic stresses are about one fifth smaller
(Section~\ref{sec:decay}). Within 12~\AA{} of the interface the shape
differs as well, because a continuum does not describe the atomic structure
of the boundary.

\subsection{How the stress falls with distance}
\label{sec:decay}

Fig.~\ref{fig:decay} gives the profiles along the chosen directions. The
atomistic values quoted here are for shells 5--10~\AA{} from the surface;
closer in, the narrow cones along the field hold only 15--20 atoms per shell,
and the values scatter by up to 25~MPa. Along the field the normal stress is
compressive, about 126~MPa, which is close to the
147~MPa estimate of Ref.~\cite{Friha2024JMMM}. The agreement is expected,
because $\varepsilon$ was derived from that estimate. Across the field the
normal stress is tensile, about 75~MPa. At the sides of the
inclusion, near the plane $x=0$, the stress along the field is also large:
as the inclusion lengthens, it pulls the aluminium next to it along, and
$\sigma_{xx}$ right at the surface there reaches 121~MPa. The shear
stress on the slip planes reaches 28~MPa in the lobes.

One radius from the surface ($r=2R$) the normal stress along the field falls
to about 54~MPa and the shear stress in the lobes to about 19~MPa; two radii away ($r=3R$) they are about
18 and 7~MPa (single-sphere solution).

From $d\approx$~10~\AA{} on, the atomistic stresses equal the elastic
solution for the same cell times 0.80 (0.80 to 0.94 for the
different directions); after this scaling they differ from it by
2.5\% on average. The atomic displacements, which do not depend on how
the stress is computed, are reduced in the same way. The matrix therefore
deforms less than with a perfectly bonded interface. The deficit builds up
in a layer 5--8~\AA{} thick at the interface: the aluminium lattice there is
distorted, the first layer has half the bulk density, and the layer passes on
only part of the strain of the inclusion (Supplementary S2).

If the compliance of the layer does not depend on particle size, the
weakening scales inversely with the radius and would be about 0.02\% for
a particle of radius 2.5~$\mu$m, so the elastic solution is used below for
real particles. This is untested: one inclusion radius and one interface were
computed, at 0~K, and sliding at the interface at room temperature would
weaken the field whatever the particle size.

The solution for the cell differs from the single-sphere solution because of
the cubic elasticity of aluminium, the fixed cell size and the neighbouring
inclusions. Their shear stresses in the lobes differ by at most 2~MPa, while
the normal stresses in the cell are 13--17\% larger at the surface and
30--44\% larger at $r=2R$. The volume fraction of inclusions in the alloy,
about 0.25\%, is more than ten times smaller than in the cell, so the far
field in Fig.~\ref{fig:decay}d is shown for a single sphere.

The field of a single sphere depends only on $r/R$, so it carries over to
inclusions of real size: for a particle 5~$\mu$m across the distances in
Fig.~\ref{fig:decay} are multiplied by a thousand (upper scale). What
follows depends on the yield stress of the alloy, and the experimental papers
give different values. Ref.~\cite{Friha2024JMMM} takes 40--60~MPa, a handbook
value for commercially pure aluminium and lean Al--Mg--Si alloys; its
samples began to deform plastically on loading at about 42~MPa.
Refs.~\cite{Skvortsov2019JAP,Pshonkin2025ZL} give 120~MPa, and 151~MPa was
measured on samples after the creep tests \cite{Pshonkin2025ZL}. For a yield
stress of 40--60~MPa the equivalent stress exceeds it out to
$r\approx\text{1.9--2.2}R$, in a shell about one radius thick
(2.2--3.1~$\mu$m for a 5~$\mu$m particle). For 120--151~MPa the
shell is thin: it reaches only $r\approx\text{1.02--1.06}R$.

For slip, what matters is the largest shear stress on the twelve slip
systems of aluminium (a slip system is a slip plane together with a slip
direction in it). It is compared with the critical resolved shear stress,
the yield stress divided by the Taylor factor 3.06 \cite{StollerZinkle2000},
which accounts for the different orientations of the grains in a
polycrystal. A critical stress of 13--20~MPa is exceeded out to
$r\approx\text{2.2--2.6}R$, and one of 39--49~MPa out to
$r\approx\text{1.1--1.5}R$. Ref.~\cite{Skvortsov2019JAP} estimated the
plastic zone as about 6~$\mu$m assuming that the stress falls as $1/r$; with
the $r^{-3}$ decay the same estimate gives about 2~$\mu$m. Where the stress
exceeds the yield stress, the elastic solution overestimates it: in the
alloy, dislocations already present would start to move and part of the
stress would relax. The atomistic calculation does not test this: its matrix
is pure aluminium without pre-existing dislocations, and at 0~K no new ones
form in it even at a lobe shear stress of 306~MPa (strain
$10\varepsilon$, Supplementary S2).

\subsection{The force on dislocations}

A shear stress $\tau$ exerts on a dislocation with Burgers vector
$b=2.86$~\AA{} a force $\tau b$ per unit length, directed along the slip plane
(the Peach--Koehler force \cite{HirthLothe1982}). The dislocation moves if this
force exceeds what holds it back: the lattice resistance (the Peierls stress,
the least stress at which a straight dislocation in a pure crystal at 0~K
moves to the next position), solute atoms and other dislocations.

Fig.~\ref{fig:glide} shows the shear stress of the inclusion field averaged
along a straight line on which an edge dislocation can lie. The line runs
parallel to $y$ at a height $h$ above the inclusion surface. The dislocation is as long as the cell period along $y$,
12.4~nm. The stress changes sign when $x$ is replaced by $-x$: on one side of
the inclusion it pushes the dislocation forward, on the other side back. The averaged stress is largest,
5.8~MPa, at $h=$~10~\AA. This is below the Peierls stress of the
potential, about 9~MPa (dashed lines in Fig.~\ref{fig:glide}). On the short part of the line right
above the inclusion the stress is larger, up to 12.2~MPa, and this part
could bow out even when the average is too small to move the whole line. The
line average depends on the spacing of the inclusions in the model
(12.4~nm along the line) and does not carry over directly to the alloy.

In an Al--Mg--Si alloy dislocations are also held by Mg and Si atoms and by
precipitates, fine particles of Mg--Si compounds inside the grains. The
critical shear stress of the alloy, from its yield stress, is
13--20~MPa for the soft alloy and 39--49~MPa for the stronger one.
In the single-sphere solution the field exceeds it only next to the
inclusion, out to $r\approx\text{2.2--2.6}R$ and 1.1--1.5$R$
respectively. Farther out the field is below this value and cannot by itself push dislocations past their obstacles; it can
only speed up or slow down their thermally activated motion, in which thermal
vibrations of the atoms carry them past the obstacles (see the creep
estimate below).

Whether the field moves a dislocation was checked directly (Fig.~\ref{fig:disl}).
An aluminium slab contains the same held inclusion and a straight edge
dislocation whose line runs through the whole slab along $y$, on the slip
plane 12~\AA{} above the inclusion (Supplementary S6). In aluminium such a
dislocation splits into two partial dislocations with a ribbon about
9.2~\AA{} wide between them, where the stacking of the atomic
planes is faulted. With the inclusion unstrained, the dislocation lies,
after energy minimisation, 20.4~\AA{} from the inclusion centre along
$x$, where the line-averaged shear stress of the field is about
4.6~MPa. When the inclusion is given the strain~(\ref{eq:strain}),
the whole dislocation line moves 0.78~\AA{} away from it on the slip
plane. The dislocation moves through the
lattice in steps of $b/2=1.43$~\AA, and each step requires crossing an
energy barrier. The shift is less than one step, and the dislocation does
not take the step. When the
strain is removed, the dislocation returns to within 0.06~\AA{} of
where it was: in this calculation at 0~K the shift is elastic and
reversible. The line-averaged field on the dislocation is about half the
Peierls stress of the potential, about 9~MPa (Supplementary S6). Where the
resistance to motion is lower, for example at room temperature, the same
field could move the dislocation further; in the alloy, Mg and Si atoms and
precipitates also hold dislocations, and the resistance there is higher
(13--49~MPa).

\subsection{An estimate for creep}

The link between the stress field and the creep rate is estimated with a model
of thermally activated glide \cite{KocksArgonAshby1975,CaillardMartin2003}.
The estimate applies to creep while the field is on and the stresses around
the inclusions exist; the observed increase was measured after the field was
removed (see the Discussion).
A dislocation passes its obstacles with the help of thermal fluctuations. A
shear stress $\tau$ makes forward jumps $\exp(V^*\tau/k_{\mathrm B}T)$ times
more likely and backward jumps less likely by the same factor; $V^*$ is the
activation volume, a measure of how strongly the barrier depends on stress,
and $k_{\mathrm B}T$ is the thermal energy. If dislocations around an
inclusion meet stresses of both signs equally often, their mean velocity grows
by the factor $\cosh(V^*\tau/k_{\mathrm B}T)$, the mean of the factors
$\exp(V^*\tau/k_{\mathrm B}T)$ and $\exp(-V^*\tau/k_{\mathrm B}T)$ for the two
signs. The excess of this factor over one, integrated over the matrix around
one inclusion and multiplied by the number of inclusions per unit volume,
$f/V_p$, gives the relative increase in creep rate (derived in
Supplementary S5),
\begin{equation}
H=\frac{f}{V_p}\int_{r>R}
\Bigl\langle\cosh\frac{V^*\tau_s(\mathbf r)}{k_{\mathrm B}T}-1\Bigr\rangle
\,\mathrm dV ,
\label{eq:creep}
\end{equation}
where $f$ is the volume fraction of inclusions, $V_p$ the volume of one
inclusion, $\tau_s$ the shear stress on slip system $s$ (a slip plane together
with a slip direction in it), and the angle
brackets an average over the twelve slip systems and over grain orientations.
The elastic field of a single sphere goes into the integral (the atomistic
weakening is taken as negligible for micron-size particles,
Section~\ref{sec:decay}); it depends only
on $r/R$, so $H$ does not depend on particle size. The values used are
$T=300$~K and $f=0.246\%$; the latter corresponds to 0.35~wt\% of inclusions
\cite{Friha2024JMMM}.

In Fig.~\ref{fig:creep} the solid curve shows the amplitude $\tau_m$ needed for
the 25\% increase in creep rate observed after holding in the field
\cite{Skvortsov2019JAP} at different $V^*$ if the shear stress
falls from the inclusion surface as $\tau_m(R/r)^3$; this amplitude falls as
the activation volume grows. The dashed curve shows the amplitude $\tau_m$ for
which such a field gives the same increase as the computed field
(Supplementary S5). The curves cross at $V^*\approx23\,b^3$. At $V^*=70\,b^3$, measured for
an Al--Mg--Si alloy \cite{Soula2022JALCOM}, the computed field would raise the
rate $10^8$ times; the model is then outside its range, and a much smaller
strain would be enough for the observed 25\%. Because $H$ depends only on the
product $V^*\varepsilon$, at $V^*=70\,b^3$ an inclusion strain of about
0.065\% is needed. The measured magnetostriction of Fe--Al alloys does not
exceed 0.01\% \cite{Hall1959,BormioNunes2012}, six to seven times less.

\section{Discussion}

The calculation gives the stresses that an inclusion of a given shape
produces in aluminium. The magnetic side remains open: the magnetostriction
of the inclusions themselves has not been measured. Alloys with these
inclusions show magnetic hysteresis
\cite{Skvortsov2019JAP,Friha2024JMMM,Pshonkin2025ZL}, that is, their
magnetisation depends on the field history. According to
Ref.~\cite{Skvortsov2019JAP} the particles are superparamagnetic above about
250~K: they are small enough for thermal fluctuations to flip their
magnetisation. The phase of the inclusions is identified differently: X-ray
diffraction points to Al$_{13}$Fe$_4$ \cite{Friha2024JMMM,Pshonkin2025ZL},
while electron microscopy and M\"ossbauer spectroscopy point to FeAl and
Fe$_3$Al alloys \cite{Skvortsov2019JAP}. Strong ferromagnetism has not been
established for bulk Al$_{13}$Fe$_4$ \cite{Albedah2015Mossbauer,Avers2025Crystals},
and the magnetostriction of Fe--Al alloys does not exceed 0.01\%
\cite{Hall1959,BormioNunes2012}.

If the real strain of the inclusions is close to the magnetostriction of
Fe--Al alloys, the stresses are twenty times smaller than computed and do
not reach the yield stress. From the single-sphere solution, the shear
stress at the particle surface exceeds the critical value once the inclusion
strain is above 0.028--0.043\% for the soft alloy
and 0.084--0.105\% for the stronger one, and the
equivalent stress exceeds the yield stress above
0.045--0.067\% and 0.134--0.168\%
respectively. These are strains of the inclusion inside the matrix; the free
magnetostriction of an elastic particle would have to be about one and a
half times larger (Section~\ref{sec:strain}). If the estimates of 560 and
650~MPa hold (a strain of 0.74--0.86\%), the equivalent stress would exceed
even a yield stress of 120--151~MPa out to $r\approx\text{2.2--2.6}R$.

The inclusion is held in its given shape, so its own elasticity does not
enter the problem. The potential describes the
elasticity of aluminium well but overestimates the lattice of Al$_{13}$Fe$_4$,
which is why the inclusion is held at the measured lattice. The calculation is
static (energy minimisation without thermal motion of the atoms) and done at
0~K for one inclusion of one size (radius 2.5~nm) in a regular array;
the elastic solution for the same cell includes the field of the neighbouring
inclusions. The matrix is pure aluminium without Mg and Si atoms,
precipitates or pre-existing dislocations, so the yield stress of the alloy
is taken from experiments \cite{Friha2024JMMM,Skvortsov2019JAP,Pshonkin2025ZL}
and plastic flow near the inclusion is not modelled.

The main limitation concerns the persistence of the effect. In the experiments creep is
measured after the field is removed, whereas the elastic stress of
magnetostriction disappears with the field. If the field moves dislocations
near the inclusions, the displaced dislocations may stay in their new positions
after the field is removed; the computed shell where the stress exceeds the
yield stress shows where to look for such changes.
In the one case computed, a straight dislocation at 0~K, the dislocation
returned when the strain was removed; the calculation does not show whether
such a rearrangement of defects persists in the alloy or how it affects
subsequent creep.

\section{Conclusions}

At the given magnetostrictive strain of 0.194\% the aluminium matrix around
an Al$_{13}$Fe$_4$ inclusion deforms elastically. In the static atomistic
calculation at 0~K no dislocations form even at ten times the strain, and a
dislocation above the inclusion shifts by 0.78~\AA{} and returns when
the strain is removed. Around the inclusion of radius 2.5~nm the matrix is
compressed along the field by about 126~MPa and stretched across
it by about 75~MPa, and the shear stress on the slip planes
reaches 28~MPa. Because of a compliant layer of distorted
aluminium at the interface the atomistic field is about 80\% of the
elastic solution; for micron-size particles this weakening is probably
negligible, but one inclusion radius at 0~K does not test this.

Plastic deformation is possible only in a zone next to the particle where
the elastic stress exceeds the yield stress. The field falls as $(R/r)^3$,
and this zone is about one radius thick for a yield stress of 40--60~MPa
(2.2--3.1~$\mu$m for a 5~$\mu$m particle) and shrinks to a thin
layer at the surface for 120--151~MPa. Plastic flow in this zone was not
computed atomistically. In a model of thermally activated glide this field,
if it acted during creep, would give the observed increase in creep rate
for an activation volume of about 23$\,b^3$; at the measured
$70\,b^3$ this needs a strain of 0.065\%, six to seven times the
measured magnetostriction of Fe--Al alloys. The calculation does not explain
why the effect persists after the field is removed.
